Which is greener?

Ibuprofen is one of the most used medicines in the world. Here are two real ways to make it. Both give the same pure ibuprofen.

Route A

  • Steps6 reaction steps
  • Atoms used40% of the atoms in the reactants end up in the ibuprofen.
  • Extra chemicalsAluminium chloride is used up in the first step. It becomes a large amount of aluminium waste that must be treated.
  • HazardsUses several corrosive reagents, including aluminium chloride.

Route B

  • Steps3 reaction steps
  • Atoms used77% of the atoms in the reactants end up in the ibuprofen. The only by-product is acetic acid, which can be collected and sold.
  • Extra chemicalsCatalysts speed up each step. They are recovered and reused.
  • HazardsUses hydrogen fluoride, a very toxic and corrosive acid. It is kept in a closed system and reused.
Decide

With a partner, choose the route you think is greener. Write one reason. There is no penalty for changing your mind later.

Three ways to judge sustainability

Part 1 of 5

Green chemistry is about design

Green chemistry is the design of chemical products and processes that are safer and more sustainable than the ones used before.

Chemists Paul Anastas and John Warner wrote twelve principles of green chemistry. VCE Chemistry uses seven of them.

The key word is design. A greener process prevents a problem before it starts, so nothing needs cleaning up afterwards.

Sustainable development and the SDGs

Sustainable development meets the needs of people today without stopping future generations from meeting their own needs.

Atom economy: a first look

Atom economy = molar mass of the desired producttotal molar mass of all reactants × 100%
Ethanol from ethene and water: C2H4(g) + H2O(g) → C2H5OH(g)
Atom economy = 46.028.0 + 18.0 × 100% = 100%
Every reactant atom ends up in the ethanol. Addition reactions like this always have an atom economy of 100%.

Key words

Green chemistry
The design of chemical products and processes that are safer and more sustainable than the ones used before.
Atom economy
The percentage of the reactants' mass that ends up in the product you want.
Catalysis
Using a catalyst, a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up.
Design for degradation
Making products that break down into harmless substances after use.
Design for energy efficiency
Making a process use as little energy as possible.
Designing safer chemicals
Making a product do its job with as little toxicity as possible.
Prevention of wastes
Avoiding waste in the first place, instead of treating it later.
Use of renewable feedstocks
Making products from raw materials that can be replaced quickly, mainly plants.
Feedstock
The raw material a process starts from.
By-product
A substance made in a reaction that is not the product you want.
Sustainable development
Meeting the needs of people today without stopping future generations from meeting theirs.
SDGs
The UN's 17 Sustainable Development Goals, to be reached by 2030.
Circular economy
A system where products and wastes are recovered and used again, instead of thrown away.
Watch out

"Green" does not just mean "uses plants". A process can use a renewable feedstock and still waste energy or make toxic by-products.

Watch out

A catalyst is not a reactant. It is not used up, so it does not count in atom economy.
