Kai Needs Firewood Before Dark

30-minute task, 30 minutes. You make: a page of calculations and a classification
  1. Part A: Kai's axe head has a mass of 1.8 kg. The first swing lands at 4 m/s, the next at 8 m/s. Calculate the kinetic energy of each swing using KE = ½mv². The speed doubled. Did the kinetic energy double?
  2. Part B: a heavier 3.6 kg axe can only be swung at 4 m/s. Calculate its kinetic energy. Which is the better upgrade: a heavier axe or a faster swing?
  3. Part C: classify the energy at each stage of the watermill (pond water, falling water, turning wheel, millstone) as kinetic or potential, and name the type from Table 7.1.

Square the speed first, then multiply by the mass, then halve it.

Hand in

  • Part B: KE value and a comparison
  • Part C: four stages classified and named

Stuck? Hints come one at a time.

  1. Write the formula first: KE = ½mv². Then write the numbers underneath it.
  2. Square the speed first. 4² = 16 and 8² = 64.
  3. Part A: ½ × 1.8 × 16 and ½ × 1.8 × 64. Compare the two answers: did it double or more than double?
  4. Part B: ½ × 3.6 × 16. Compare with the 8 m/s answer from Part A.
  5. Part C: anything held up high is gravitational potential; anything moving is kinetic.

Not sure how to set it out? See a worked example (different numbers).

  1. A different example: a 2 kg rock at 3 m/s.
  2. Formula: KE = ½mv²
  3. Substitute: KE = ½ × 2 × 3²
  4. Square first: 3² = 9, so KE = ½ × 2 × 9
  5. Multiply: KE = 9 J

    Answer: v ≈ 5.66 m/s

Answers: check your work after you've finished

Check and remember

Test yourself, then read the big ideas. If you got something wrong, go back to that step.

What to remember from Lesson 2

  • Kinetic energy is the energy of movement. It depends on mass and speed.
  • Potential energy is stored energy, waiting to be released: gravitational, elastic, chemical and nuclear.
  • Thermal, light, sound and electrical energy are all kinetic.
  • KE = ½mv². Doubling the speed makes the kinetic energy four times bigger.
  • GPE = mgh, with g = 10 N/kg. Convert grams to kilograms and centimetres to metres first.
  • When something falls with no energy lost, its GPE at the top equals its KE at the bottom.

Key words

Kinetic energy
Energy something has because it is moving.
Potential energy
Stored energy, waiting to be released.
Gravitational potential energy
Energy stored because of height above the ground.
Mass
How much matter an object contains, in kg.
Joule (J)
The unit of energy.
GPE = mgh
Mass (kg) × g (10 N/kg) × height (m). The answer is in joules.
KE = ½mv²
Half × mass (kg) × speed (m/s) squared. The answer is in joules.

Mapping Heat Through Kai's Hut

30-minute task, 30 minutes. You make: an observation sheet

Aim: to observe convection and explain all three heat transfers in Kai's hut.

  1. Predict: the hut has one small window and a tall chimney. Will warm air spread evenly, or stack in layers? Give a reason.
  2. Observe: in the simulation's Convection tab (turn on particle trails), or the teacher's demo, sketch how the particles move as they warm and cool. Use arrows.
  3. Explain, using particle theory: what happens to the iron poker, why the wooden handle stays cool, and how the fire's warmth reaches Kai's face.

Use the word 'density' when you explain why warm water or air rises.

Hand in

  • Prediction with a reason
  • Labelled sketch with arrows
  • Explanation using conduction, convection and radiation

📓 Do this in your book. Write each question or heading, then your answer.

Stuck? Hints come one at a time.

  1. Predict: think about what warm air does. Does it stay by the fire or rise?
  2. Sketch: draw arrows up above the heat, across the top, and down the cool side. That loop is a convection current.
  3. Poker: the iron particles vibrate more and bump their neighbours. Iron is a conductor.
  4. Handle: wood is an insulator, so the vibrations are passed along badly. It's not that wood is cold.
  5. Face: the warmth arrives as waves. No particles are needed. That's radiation.

Answers: check your work after you've finished

Test yourself, then read the big ideas. If you got something wrong, go back to that step.

What to remember from Lesson 3

  • Heat always moves from hot to cold.
  • Conduction: in solids, vibrating particles pass energy to their neighbours.
  • Convection: in liquids and gases, hot, less dense fluid rises and cooler fluid sinks.
  • Radiation: heat travels as waves and needs no particles at all.
  • Conductors (metals) let heat through easily; insulators (wood, plastic, air) resist it.

Key words

Conduction
Heat passing through a material as particles bump their neighbours.
Convection
Heat carried by a liquid or gas as its particles flow.
Radiation
Heat travelling as waves, with no particles needed.
Conductor
A material that lets heat pass through easily.
Insulator
A material that resists heat passing through.
Density
How much mass is packed into a space.

  2. The panel: 500 J of sunlight hits it, and 100 J of electrical energy reaches the battery. Calculate the efficiency.

E = VIt is beyond the Year 8 textbook. It previews the Electricity unit, where Kai wires up the same cabin.

E = VItE = energy (J) · V = voltage (V) · I = current (A) · t = time (s)Efficiency = useful energy outtotal energy in × 100%1 kJ = 1,000 J · 1 min = 60 s · 1 h = 3,600 s

    One step. In full sun, Kai's panel produces 20 V and 5 A. How much electrical energy does it produce in 10 seconds?

    Answer: 1,000 J
    1. E = VIt
    2. E = 20 × 5 × 10
    3. E = 1,000 J
  2. 2

    Efficiency. In those same 10 seconds, 5,000 J of sunlight hits the panel. How efficient is the panel?

    Answer: 20%
    1. Efficiency = useful outtotal in × 100%
    2. = 1,0005,000 × 100%
    3. = 20%
  3. 3

    A cloudy day. Cloud cuts the sunlight to 3,000 J every 10 seconds, and the current drops to 2 A at 20 V. What is the panel's efficiency now?

    Answer: About 13.3%
    1. Electrical energy out: E = VIt = 20 × 2 × 10 = 400 J
    2. Efficiency = 4003,000 × 100%
    3. ≈ 13.3%
  4. 4

    Hardest. The panel is 20% efficient, and 500 J of sunlight hits it every second. How long will it take to generate 100 kJ of electrical energy? Give your answer in seconds and in minutes.

    Answer: 1,000 s, about 16.7 minutes (16 min 40 s)
    1. Useful energy each second = 20% of 500 J = 0.20 × 500 = 100 J
    2. Convert the target: 100 kJ = 100,000 J
    3. Time = 100,000 J100 J per second = 1,000 s
    4. In minutes: 1,00060 ≈ 16.7 minutes (16 min 40 s)
  5. 5

    Stretch. Kai's 4 W LED lamp runs for 5 hours each night. The battery only gives back 80% of the energy put into it. How long must the panel run in full sun (500 J of sunlight per second, 20% efficient) to power the lamp for one night?

    Answer: 900 s, which is 15 minutes
    1. Lamp energy: E = power × time = 4 W × (5 × 3,600 s) = 72,000 J
    2. The battery returns only 80%, so it must store 72,0000.80 = 90,000 J
    3. The panel gives 0.20 × 500 = 100 J every second
    4. Time = 90,000100 = 900 s = 15 minutes

Answers: check your work after you've finished

Fire 15%, panel 20%. Fire Sankey: 8,000 J in, 1,200 J useful heat, 6,800 J wasted (heat to the air, light, sound). The panel is more efficient, but the fire works at night and in cloudy weather, cooks food, and heats the hut directly.

Test yourself, then read the big ideas. If you got something wrong, go back to that step.

What to remember from Lesson 4

  • A transfer moves energy between objects. A transformation changes its type.
  • Energy is never created or destroyed. 'Lost' energy usually ends up as heat and sound.
  • Efficiency = useful energy outtotal energy in × 100%. It can never be more than 100%.
  • In a Sankey diagram, arrow widths show how much energy each part carries.
  • A pendulum swaps GPE and KE, and slowly loses energy to the air as heat.

Key words

Transfer
Energy moving from one object to another.
Transformation
Energy changing from one type to another.
Efficiency
How much of the input energy becomes useful energy.
Sankey diagram
An arrow diagram where widths show energy amounts.
System
A set of parts working together to do a job.
Conservation of energy
Energy can't be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed.

⭐ Two tricky ideas

Write your answers in your book first, then check.

  1. 1

    A thermometer shows that the bathroom tiles and the bath mat are both at 16 °C. Explain why the tiles feel much colder on bare feet.

    Answer: They are at the same temperature, so the difference is how fast heat leaves your feet. Your feet (about 33 °C) are warmer than both, so heat flows from your feet into the floor. Tiles are a good conductor, so they carry heat away from your skin quickly and your feet cool fast. The mat traps air and is an insulator, so heat leaves slowly. Your skin senses how fast it is losing heat, not the temperature of the floor.
  2. 2

    Kai drops a pinecone from 0.45 m and it lands at 3 m/s. Ignoring air resistance, from what height would it need to fall to land at 6 m/s? Show your working.

    Answer: 1.8 m. KE = ½mv², so doubling the landing speed needs 2² = 4 times the kinetic energy. All of that comes from GPE = mgh, so the pinecone needs 4 times the height: 4 × 0.45 = 1.8 m.

Station: Evaluate your insulation practical

About 10 minutes. You need: your Lesson 8 practical report

Three ideas to learn first.

  • Reliable results come out about the same when the test is repeated. To make results more reliable, repeat each test (at least three times) and average. Repeats show up a one-off mistake, and averaging reduces the effect of random errors.
  • Valid means the test really measures what it claims to: it is a fair test, where only the independent variable changes. Controlling another variable makes the test more valid.
  • A result that can't be right. If a reading doesn't fit the pattern, or would break a rule of science, it is an error: misread, miscopied, or a mistake in the method. Say which rule it breaks, leave it out of any average, and repeat that measurement.
  1. Practice first. Mia drops a tennis ball from 100 cm and measures how high it bounces. Her four trials: 62 cm, 60 cm, 105 cm, 61 cm. Which result can't be right? Explain using energy.
  2. Calculate Mia's average bounce height, leaving out that result.
  3. Suggest one change to Mia's method that would make her average more trustworthy. Explain how it helps.
  4. Now your own data. Look at your Lesson 8 results table. Is there any reading that doesn't fit the pattern of the others? If there isn't, write how you checked.
  5. Name one thing that wasn't exactly the same for every cup in your virtual practical, and explain how it could have changed your results. Look closely at your 0-minute readings and at the wrappings.
  6. Suggest one improvement to your practical. Say whether it improves reliability or validity, and why.

An improvement needs two parts: what you would change, and how that makes the conclusion better. 'Be more careful' earns nothing.
