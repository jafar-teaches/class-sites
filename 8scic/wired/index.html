Charge comes from atoms

All matter is made of atoms. The nucleus holds positive protons and neutral neutrons. Negative electrons sit around the outside.

An object with equal numbers of protons and electrons is neutral. It has no overall charge.

Count the charges

The object has…So it is…
more protons than electronspositive
the same number of protons and electronsneutral
more electrons than protonsnegative

How rubbing makes a charge

  1. 1Two neutral objects are rubbed together
  2. 2Electrons move from one object to the other
  3. 3The object that gains electrons becomes negative
  4. 4The object that loses electrons becomes positive
  5. 5Like charges repel; unlike charges attract

Only electrons move. Protons are stuck in the nucleus. Rubbing moves charge. It never makes new charge.

Which way is the force?

Push apart (repel)

  • Two positive objects

Pull together (attract)

  • A positive and a negative object
  • A charged object and a neutral object
  • A charged balloon and a wall

Why the hair stood on end

  1. 1The machine's belt builds up charge on the dome
  2. 2Charge spreads over the volunteer, out to every hair
  3. 3Every strand has the same type of charge
  4. 4Like charges repel, so the strands push apart

The pie tins fly off for the same reason. Each tin gets the same charge as the dome.

Fields: forces without touching

The electrostatic force is an indirect force. It acts without the objects touching.

A field is the area around an object where another object feels a force. Magnets, charges and planets all have fields.

Field lines show the direction of the force. Where the lines are closer together, the force is stronger.

Field lines: charges and a magnet

Lines point away from a positive charge and towards a negative charge. Around a magnet they run from N to S.

Think about it

Field lines are a model

The lines are not real strings. The field fills all the space around the object. The lines just show direction and strength.

Sparks and lightning

  1. 1Charge builds up on an object or cloud
  2. 2Air is an insulator, so charge cannot cross at first
  3. 3Enough charge builds up for the air to break down
  4. 4Charge rushes across the gap very fast
  5. 5We see a spark, or lightning on a huge scale

A spark is an electrostatic shock: the rapid movement of charged particles from one object to another.

Worked example: the jumper spark

You pull off a wool jumper on a dry day. Then a tiny spark jumps from a metal door handle to your finger. Explain why.

  1. Wool pulls on electrons more strongly than skin does.Some materials attract electrons more than others.
  2. Electrons move from your skin onto the jumper.Only electrons move.
  3. Your body is now positive. The jumper is now negative.Lose electrons: positive. Gain electrons: negative.
  4. Near the handle, electrons in the metal are pulled strongly towards your finger.Unlike charges attract. Metal lets electrons move easily.
  5. Electrons jump the small air gap. That fast movement is the spark.Dry air lets the charge build up first.

Answer: Electrons moved from skin to wool, so your body became positive. Electrons then jumped from the handle to your finger as a spark (an electrostatic shock).

Charged objects attract neutral ones

A charged balloon sticks to a neutral wall. How can that be? The wall has no overall charge.

The negative balloon repels electrons in the wall's surface a tiny distance away. The surface nearest the balloon is left slightly positive. This is called induced charge.

Induced charge

  1. 1A negative balloon comes near a neutral wall
  2. 2Electrons in the wall's surface are pushed slightly away
  3. 3The surface nearest the balloon becomes slightly positive
  4. 4The nearby positive part attracts the balloon strongly
  5. 5The far negative part repels weakly, so attraction wins

In the real world

Lightning safety

Lightning takes the easiest path to the ground. In a storm, go inside a building or a hard-topped car. Stay away from hilltops and lone trees.

From history

Benjamin Franklin

In the 1740s Franklin named the two charges positive and negative. He also showed that lightning is a giant spark. Because of his names, circuit diagrams still show current flowing from + to −.

Explain

A circuit is a closed path

A circuit is a closed path for transferring electrical energy.

Charge can only flow if there is a complete loop. It flows out of the battery, through the parts, and back to the battery.

Three things every circuit needs

  1. An energy source, such as a cell or battery.
  2. Components that transform the electrical energy, such as a bulb, motor or heater. A component that uses electrical energy is called a load.
  3. Wires that join everything into a complete loop, back to the energy source.

Open and closed circuits

Close the switch and charge flows. Make a gap anywhere and the flow stops everywhere.

Open the animation full screen ↗

Open or closed?

Closed circuit

  • A complete loop
  • Current flows
  • The bulb lights

Open circuit

  • A gap somewhere, such as an open switch
  • No current flows
  • The bulb is off

Bulb (lamp)Transforms electrical energy into light and heat.

Five rules for drawing circuits

  1. Use a sharp pencil and a ruler.
  2. Draw symbols on the straight sides, not at the corners.
  3. Join the symbols with straight wires that turn at 90°.
  4. Make sure the wires do not cross.
  5. Check the loop: trace from the battery, through each part, and back.

Where does the energy go?

  1. 1The battery's chemical energy gives the charge electrical energy
  2. 2The charge carries the energy along the wire
  3. 3In the bulb, the charge transfers its energy
  4. 4The bulb transforms it into light and heat
  5. 5The charge flows back to the battery for more energy

The charge goes round and round. It is not used up. Only the energy is passed on.

Transfer or transform?

Energy transfer

  • Energy moves from one place to another
  • The charge carries energy from the battery to the bulb

Energy transformation

  • Energy changes from one form to another
  • Chemical to electrical in the battery
  • Electrical to light and heat in the bulb
Think about it

So why does a battery go flat?

Not because it runs out of charge. A battery goes flat when its chemicals are used up. Then it can no longer give the charge energy.

Current: the flow of charge

Current is the rate of flow of charge. It tells you how much charge passes a point each second.

The symbol for current is I. It is measured in amperes, or amps (A).

On circuit diagrams, current flows from the + end of the battery, round the circuit, to the − end.

Current is the same everywhere

Push one ball into a full pipe and one ball comes out the other end. No balls are lost.

Open the animation full screen ↗

Measuring current: the ammeter

An ammeter measures current. It goes in series, which means it is part of the loop.

All the charge must flow through it. Connect the red terminal on the + side.

Voltage: the push

Voltage (also called potential difference) is how much energy each bit of charge gains or loses between two points.

The symbol for voltage is V. It is measured in volts (V).

The battery gives the charge energy. Each part of the circuit takes some of that energy as the charge passes through.

Voltage as a rollercoaster

The battery lifts the charge up the hill. The charge loses that energy on the way down, in the bulb.

Measuring voltage: the voltmeter

A voltmeter measures voltage. It goes in parallel, which means across a part, with one lead on each side.

It compares the energy of the charge before and after that part.

The two meters

Ammeter

  • Measures current (I)
  • Unit: amps (A)
  • Goes in series, in the loop

Voltmeter

  • Measures voltage (V)
  • Unit: volts (V)
  • Goes in parallel, across a part

Resistance: a first look

Resistance is how much a part opposes the flow of charge. Its symbol is R, and it is measured in ohms (Ω).

More resistance in the loop means less current. That is why two bulbs gave a smaller current than one. You will learn more next lesson.

Cells add up

Cells joined one after another add their voltages. Two 1.5 V cells make a 3.0 V battery.

What resistance is

Resistance is how much a part opposes the flow of charge. Its symbol is R. It is measured in ohms (Ω).

Every part of a circuit has some resistance. A resistor is a part made to add an exact amount of resistance.

Why the bulb went dim

  1. 1A resistor is added to the loop
  2. 2Total resistance in the loop goes up
  3. 3With the same voltage, less current flows
  4. 4The current is smaller everywhere in the loop
  5. 5Less energy reaches the bulb each second, so it dims

Resistance makes heat

When charge flows through resistance, some electrical energy is transformed into heat. Heaters, toasters and kettles use this on purpose.

Conductors, such as copper, have low resistance. Insulators, such as rubber, have very high resistance.

A wire has more resistance when it is…

  • longer: the charge must flow through more wire
  • thinner: there is less room for the charge to flow
  • made of a poorer conductor: nichrome has much more resistance than copper
  • hotter: most metals resist more as they warm up

Ohm's law

Georg Ohm found how voltage, current and resistance are linked. We write it as V = I × R.

For a fixed resistor, the current is proportional to the voltage. Double the voltage and the current doubles too.

Ohmic

If the voltage across the resistor increases, then the current will increase in proportion, because the resistor's resistance stays the same and I = VR. Double the voltage and the current doubles. A bigger push makes more charge flow each second.

  2. Put the voltmeter across the resistor only.
  3. Ask your teacher to check the circuit before you switch on.

    Suggested solution

    The resistor's graph is a straight line. The bulb's graph curves and flattens out at higher voltages. As the voltage goes up, the bulb's filament gets hotter. Hotter metal has more resistance. Evaluation

  1. The cells gave less than 1.5 V each when current flowed. Why does this not spoil the test?
    Suggested solution

    We measure the real voltage across the resistor with the voltmeter. We use that measured value in every calculation and on the graph. So it does not matter that 4 cells gave about 5.36 V instead of 6.0 V. Each point still pairs a real voltage with its real current.

  2. Were your three readings close together? What does that tell you about your data?
    Suggested solution

    Yes, if the three currents in a row were within about 0.01 A to 0.02 A of each other. Close readings mean the results are repeatable, so the mean is reliable. If one reading is far from the other two, it is an outlier. Check it, repeat it, and leave it out of the mean.

  3. Suggest one improvement to the method and explain how it would help.
    Suggested solution

    One example: use a power pack that can be set in 1 V steps. This gives more points on the graph, so the line of best fit is more accurate. Another: use digital meters that read to 0.01 A, so readings are more precise.

Remember

  • For a fixed resistor, current is proportional to voltage.
  • R = VI stays the same for an ohmic resistor.

  • Not quite: The resistor sets the current, whatever the voltage.

How does joining bulbs in series or in parallel affect the total current from the battery?

If more bulbs are added in parallel, then the total current will increase, because each new branch adds its own current.

The rules

Series

  • The current is the same everywhere.
  • The voltages add up to the supply.
  • Resistances add: R = R₁ + R₂ + …
  • Use V = I × R for each part.

Parallel

  • Every branch gets the full supply voltage.
  • Branch currents add up to the total current.
  • 1R = 1R₁ + 1R₂ + …
  • Use V = I × R for each branch.

Worked example 1: series (full)

A 12 V battery is joined to a 2 Ω resistor and a 4 Ω resistor in series. Find the current and the voltage across each resistor.

  1. R(total) = 2 + 4 = 6 ΩIn series, resistances add.
  2. I = VR = 126 = 2 AUse the supply voltage with the total resistance.
  3. V(2 Ω) = I × R = 2 × 2 = 4 VThe same 2 A flows through each part.
  4. V(4 Ω) = 2 × 4 = 8 VThe bigger resistor takes the bigger share.
  5. Check: 4 V + 8 V = 12 VIn series, the voltages add up to the supply.

Answer: I = 2 A; 4 V across the 2 Ω and 8 V across the 4 Ω

Worked example 2: parallel branches (full)

A 12 V battery is joined to a 6 Ω branch and a 3 Ω branch in parallel. Find the current in each branch and the total current.

  1. Each branch has 12 V across it.In parallel, every branch gets the full supply voltage.
  2. I(6 Ω) = VR = 126 = 2 AUse V = I × R for one branch at a time.
  3. I(3 Ω) = 123 = 4 AThe smaller resistance takes the bigger current.
  4. Total I = 2 + 4 = 6 ABranch currents add up to the total.

Answer: 2 A and 4 A in the branches; 6 A in total

Total resistance in parallel (more paths, less resistance)

Each new branch is another path. More paths let more current through, so the total resistance goes down.

Electricity and magnetism are linked

A magnetic field is the space around a magnet where it pushes or pulls. Field lines show which way a compass would point.

In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted found that a wire carrying a current moves a compass needle. A current makes a magnetic field.

From a wire to an electromagnet

  1. 1A current flows in a wire
  2. 2A magnetic field forms in circles round the wire
  3. 3Wind the wire into a coil
  4. 4The circles add up: the coil acts like a bar magnet
  5. 5Put an iron core inside the coil
  6. 6A strong magnet you can switch on and off

Switch off the current and the field (almost) disappears. That is what makes an electromagnet useful.

Three ways to make an electromagnet stronger

  1. More turns of wire. Each turn adds its own magnetic field.
  2. More current. More current makes a stronger field from every turn.
  3. An iron core. The coil's field magnetises the iron, and the iron adds its own field.

The motor effect

  1. 1A current flows through a wire
  2. 2The wire is in a magnet's field
  3. 3The field pushes on the current: a force
  4. 4The force makes the wire move
  5. 5Keep the current flowing and it keeps moving

Current + magnetic field → force → motion. This is how every electric motor works.

The homopolar motor

Current flows from the top of the battery, down the wire and through the magnet. The magnet's field pushes on that current, so the wire spins.

Why the wire spins

The magnet does two jobs. It makes the magnetic field, and its metal coating carries the current back to the battery.

Flip the magnet and its field points the other way. The push reverses, so the wire spins the other way.

The reverse: the slow magnet

  1. 1The magnet falls down the pipe
  2. 2The magnetic field in the copper keeps changing
  3. 3A changing field makes currents flow in the copper
  4. 4These currents make their own magnetic field
  5. 5That field pushes against the falling magnet
  6. 6The magnet falls slowly

Copper is not magnetic. The slowing comes from the currents the moving magnet makes.

Motor and generator: two sides of one idea

Motor

  • Electricity in, motion out.
  • Current + magnetic field → force → motion.
  • Example: the homopolar motor, a fan, a drill.

Generator

  • Motion in, electricity out.
  • Moving magnet → changing field → current.
  • Example: the copper pipe, a wind turbine, a power station.

Spin a magnet near a coil of wire and a current flows in the coil. Almost all of our mains electricity is made this way. You will build on this in Year 9.

In 1821, Michael Faraday made the first electric motor. In 1831, he found that a moving magnet can make a current. That idea runs every power station today.

Explain

Bigger circuits, bigger dangers

The circuits in your home work like the ones you built in class. They are just bigger.

Home electricity has a much higher voltage: 240 V. That is 80 times the 3 V of two cells. It can drive a deadly current through a person.

AC and DC

Direct current (DC)

  • Flows in one direction only.
  • From batteries and phone chargers.
  • Usually low voltage, so safer.

Alternating current (AC)

  • Changes direction many times every second.
  • Mains power in homes: 240 V.
  • At 240 V it can drive a deadly current through your body.

Why mains is AC

A transformer can easily change AC to a higher or lower voltage. Very high voltage loses less energy in long power lines.

So AC is good for sending energy a long way. Many small devices change it back to low-voltage DC with a charger.

Short circuits

A short circuit happens when two wires touch when they should not. It makes a path with almost no resistance.

With almost no resistance, the current becomes huge. The wires get very hot and can start a fire.

Fault 1: a short circuit

  1. 1Active and neutral wires touch
  2. 2A path with almost no resistance forms
  3. 3The current becomes huge
  4. 4Wires heat up: fire risk
  5. 5Circuit breaker trips (or fuse melts)
  6. 6The current stops

Fuse and circuit breaker

Fuse

  • A thin wire that melts if the current is too big.
  • Used once, then replaced.

Circuit breaker

  • A switch that turns off if the current is too big.
  • Fix the problem, then switch it back on.

Fault 2: the metal case becomes live

  1. 1A loose active wire touches the metal case
  2. 2The earth wire gives an easy path to the ground
  3. 3A huge current flows to earth
  4. 4The circuit breaker trips
  5. 5The case is safe to touch

Without an earth wire, the case would stay live. Anyone touching it could get a shock.

Fault 3: current through a person

  1. 1A person touches something live
  2. 2A small current leaks through them to the ground
  3. 3Too small to trip the circuit breaker
  4. 4Safety switch notices current going in does not come back
  5. 5Safety switch cuts the power in a split second

A safety switch trips if about 30 mA (0.03 A) leaks away, within 0.3 seconds. Most trip much faster.

Some appliances, like hairdryers, have only two pins and no earth wire. They are double insulated.

Static electricity

Core

  1. A plastic rod is rubbed with wool and becomes negative. What moved, and which way?

    Answer

    Electrons moved from the wool to the rod. The rod has extra electrons (negative); the wool is left positive.

  2. An object has 10 protons and 12 electrons. Is it positive, negative or neutral?

    Answer

    Negative. It has 2 more electrons than protons.

  3. A charged balloon sticks to a neutral wall. Explain why.

    Hint

    Think about what the balloon does to the electrons in the wall.

    Answer

    A negative balloon pushes electrons in the wall's surface away. The nearby surface is left slightly positive. Unlike charges attract, so the balloon sticks.

Circuits and meters

Core

  1. Describe the energy changes in a torch.

    Answer

    Chemical energy in the battery → electrical energy carried by the charge → light and heat in the bulb.

  2. Where do you connect an ammeter and a voltmeter?

    Answer

    Ammeter in series (in the loop). Voltmeter in parallel, across the part you are measuring.

  3. An ammeter before a bulb reads 0.4 A. What does an ammeter after the bulb read?

    Answer

    0.4 A. Current is the same everywhere in one loop. The bulb transforms energy; it does not use up charge.

Core

    2. I = 124 = 3

Series circuits

Core

  1. A 9 V battery is connected to two bulbs in series. Bulb 1 has 3.5 V across it. What is the voltage across bulb 2?

    Steps
    1. Series voltages add to the supply
    2. 9 − 3.5 = 5.5
    Answer

    5.5 V

  2. A 2 Ω and a 4 Ω resistor are in series with a 12 V battery. Find the total resistance, the current, and the voltage across each resistor.

    Steps
    1. R = 2 + 4 = 6 Ω
    2. I = 126 = 2 A (the same everywhere)
    3. V across 2 Ω = 2 × 2 = 4 V
    4. V across 4 Ω = 2 × 4 = 8 V
    5. Check: 4 + 8 = 12 V
    Answer

    6 Ω; 2 A; 4 V and 8 V

  3. Resistors of 10 Ω, 20 Ω and 30 Ω are in series with a 12 V supply. Find the voltage across the 30 Ω resistor.

    Steps
    1. R = 10 + 20 + 30 = 60 Ω
    2. I = 1260 = 0.2 A
    3. V = 0.2 × 30 = 6
    Answer

    6 V

Parallel circuits

Extend

  1. In a parallel circuit the total current is 0.45 A. One branch carries 0.20 A. What does the other branch carry?

    Steps
    1. Branch currents add to the total
    2. 0.45 − 0.20 = 0.25
    Answer

    0.25 A

  2. Two 10 Ω resistors are in parallel. Find the total resistance.

    Steps
    1. 1R = 110 + 110 = 210
    2. R = 102 = 5
    Answer

    5 Ω (two equal resistors give half)

  3. A 4 Ω and a 12 Ω resistor are in parallel with a 12 V battery. Find each branch current, the total current and the total resistance. Check your answer.

    Steps
    1. Each branch gets 12 V
    2. 4 Ω branch: 124 = 3 A
    3. 12 Ω branch: 1212 = 1 A
    4. Total current = 3 + 1 = 4 A
    5. 1R = 14 + 112 = 312 + 112 = 412, so R = 3 Ω
    6. Check: 123 = 4 A
    Answer

    3 A and 1 A; total 4 A; total resistance 3 Ω

  4. Find the total resistance of 20 Ω and 30 Ω in parallel.

    Steps
    1. 1R = 120 + 130 = 360 + 260 = 560
    2. R = 605 = 12
    Answer

    12 Ω

  5. Find the total resistance of 2 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel.

    Steps
    1. 1R = 12 + 13 = 36 + 26 = 56
    2. R = 65 = 1.2
    Answer

    1.2 Ω

  6. Find the total resistance of 6 Ω, 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel.

    Steps
    1. 1R = 16 + 16 + 13 = 16 + 16 + 26 = 46
    2. R = 64 = 1.5
    Answer

    1.5 Ω

  7. Explain why the total resistance in parallel is less than the smallest branch.

    Answer

    Each new branch is another path for the current. More paths let more total current flow for the same voltage, so the total resistance is lower.

Calculation challenge

Challenge

  1. A 10 Ω resistor is connected to a 6 V supply. A second 10 Ω resistor is added in parallel. What happens to the total current?

    Steps
    1. Before: I = 610 = 0.6 A
    2. After: R = 5 Ω, so I = 65 = 1.2 A
    Answer

    It doubles, from 0.6 A to 1.2 A.

  2. Which resistor, in parallel with 12 Ω, gives a total of 4 Ω?

    Steps
    1. 14 = 112 + 1R
    2. 1R = 14 − 112 = 312 − 112 = 212
    3. R = 122 = 6
    Answer

    6 Ω

  3. A 4 Ω resistor is in series with a parallel pair of 6 Ω and 3 Ω, on a 12 V supply. Find the total resistance, the current from the supply, and the current in each branch.

    Steps
    1. Parallel pair: 1R = 16 + 13 = 36, so R = 2 Ω
    2. Total R = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω
    3. I = 126 = 2 A
    4. V across 4 Ω = 2 × 4 = 8 V, so the pair gets 12 − 8 = 4 V
    5. 6 Ω branch: 46 ≈ 0.67 A; 3 Ω branch: 43 ≈ 1.33 A
    6. Check: 0.67 + 1.33 = 2 A
    Answer

    6 Ω; 2 A; about 0.67 A and 1.33 A

Electromagnetism

Core

  1. Name three ways to make an electromagnet stronger.

    Answer

    More turns, more current, an iron core.

  2. Why does a scrapyard use an electromagnet, not a bar magnet?

    Answer

    It can be switched off to drop the car.

  3. In Practical 3, why did the number of turns have to stay the same?

    Answer

    The number of turns also changes the strength. It had to be controlled so only the current changed.

  4. Use a flowchart to explain the motor effect.

    Answer

    Current flows in a wire → the wire is in a magnetic field → the field pushes on the current (force) → the wire moves.

Electricity in the home

Core

  1. What does a circuit breaker protect, and what does a safety switch protect?

    Answer

    A circuit breaker protects the wiring (cuts off too much current). A safety switch protects people (cuts off a leak to earth).

  2. A loose active wire touches a metal toaster case. What does the earth wire do?

    Answer

    It gives an easy path to the ground. A huge current flows, so the circuit breaker trips and the case is no longer live.

  3. Why are house circuits wired in parallel?

    Answer

    Each appliance gets the full 240 V and can be switched on and off by itself. If one fails, the others keep working.

  4. What is a short circuit, and why is it dangerous?

    Answer

    A path with almost no resistance, such as two wires touching. The current becomes huge and the wires can overheat and start a fire.

Quick check

  1. Two identical bulbs are in series with a 6 V battery. What is the voltage across each?

    In series the voltages add to the supply. Two identical bulbs share it equally: 3 V each.

  2. A 6 Ω and a 3 Ω resistor are in parallel. What is the total resistance?

    1R = 16 + 13 = 36, so R = 2 Ω. It is less than the smallest branch.

  3. Which meter goes in parallel, across a bulb?

    A voltmeter compares two points, so it goes across the part.

  4. What protects a person who touches a faulty appliance?

    The leak through a person is too small for a fuse or breaker. The safety switch detects it.

  5. Rubbing a balloon on hair makes the balloon negative. Why?

    Only electrons move when objects are rubbed. The balloon gained electrons.

Remember

  • V = I × R. Write what you know, choose the formula, include units.
  • Series: same current, voltages add, resistances add.
  • Parallel: same voltage, currents add, 1R = 1R₁ + 1R₂.
  • A current makes a magnetic field; a moving magnet makes a current.
  • A circuit breaker protects the wiring. A safety switch protects people.

Watch out

  • Not quite: Current is used up by bulbs, so less comes back to the battery.

    Better: Current is the same all round a loop. Energy is transformed, not charge.

  • Not quite: In parallel, adding a branch increases the total resistance.

    Better: Each branch is another path, so the total resistance goes down.

  • Not quite: In series each bulb always gets half the battery voltage.

    Better: Only for two identical bulbs. The voltages add up to the supply voltage.

  • Not quite: A circuit breaker protects you from a shock.

    Better: A breaker protects the wiring. A safety switch protects people.
