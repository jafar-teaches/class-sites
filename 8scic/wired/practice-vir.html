Worksheet A · about 20 min · 10 questions

Matching V, I and R

NameDate

Learning intention

To understand that V, I and R in one calculation must all belong to the same part, or all to the whole circuit.

Success criteria

  • I can rearrange V = I × R to find V, I or R, and give the right unit.
  • I can change mA to A and kΩ to Ω before I calculate.
  • I can say which thing a value belongs to: the whole circuit or one part.
The rules

Three ways to write Ohm's law

  • V = I × R
  • I = VR
  • R = VI

Units

  • voltage V in volts (V)
  • current I in amps (A)
  • resistance R in ohms (Ω)
  • 1 A = 1000 mA
  • 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω

Match V, I and R

  • Whole circuit: supply voltage, total current, total resistance.
  • One part: the voltage across it, the current through it, its own resistance.

Worked example: A fan motor

A small fan motor has 6.0 V across it. The current through it is 400 mA. Find the resistance of the motor.

  1. Write what you know: V = 6.0 V, I = 400 mA, R = ?List each value with its unit.
  2. Change mA to A: I = 400 mA1000 = 0.40 AThe formula needs amps.
  3. Choose the formula: R = VIWe want R, so use R = V over I.
  4. Substitute: R = 6.0 V0.40 APut each value in with its unit.
  5. Answer: R = 15 ΩResistance is in ohms.

Which thing? All three values belong to the motor: the voltage across it, the current through it and its own resistance.

Series circuits

NameDate

Learning intention

To understand how current, voltage and resistance behave when parts are joined in one loop.

Success criteria

  • I can find the total resistance of parts in series.
  • I can use the total resistance to find the current in a series loop.
  • I can find the voltage across each part and check that they add up to the supply.
The rules

Series rules

  • The current is the same at every point in the loop.
  • The voltages across the parts add up to the supply voltage.
  • R(total) = R₁ + R₂ + …

Match V, I and R

  • Whole circuit: I = V(supply)R(total)
  • One part: V = I × R, using that part's own resistance.

Worked example: Two resistors in series

A 6.0 V battery is joined to a 4 Ω resistor and an 8 Ω resistor in series. Find the current and the voltage across each resistor.

  1. Write what you know: V = 6.0 V, R₁ = 4 Ω, R₂ = 8 ΩList each value with its unit.
  2. Total resistance: R(total) = 4 Ω + 8 Ω = 12 ΩIn series, resistances add.
  3. Current: I = VR(total) = 6.0 V12 Ω = 0.5 AWhole circuit: supply voltage with total resistance.
  4. V(R₁) = I × R₁ = 0.5 A × 4 Ω = 2 VOne part: the same 0.5 A flows through R₁.
  5. V(R₂) = 0.5 A × 8 Ω = 4 VThe bigger resistor gets the bigger share.
  6. Check: 2 V + 4 V = 6 VThe voltages add up to the supply.

Answer: I = 0.5 A; 2 V across R₁ and 4 V across R₂

Which thing? The current uses whole-circuit values (6.0 V, 12 Ω). Each voltage uses that resistor's own values.

Parallel circuits

NameDate

Learning intention

To understand how current, voltage and resistance behave when parts are joined in separate branches.

Success criteria

  • I can find the current in each branch using the full supply voltage.
  • I can add the branch currents to find the current in the main wire.
  • I can calculate the total resistance in parallel and check that it is smaller than the smallest branch.
The rules

Parallel rules

  • Every branch gets the full supply voltage.
  • The branch currents add up to the current in the main wire.
  • 1R(total) = 1R₁ + 1R₂ + …
  • R(total) is smaller than the smallest branch.

Shortcut for two resistors

  • R(total) = R₁ × R₂R₁ + R₂ (product over sum)

Worked example: Two branches

A 9.0 V battery is joined to a 6 Ω branch and an 18 Ω branch in parallel. Find the current in each branch, the total current and the total resistance.

  1. Each branch has the full 9.0 V across it.In parallel, every branch gets the supply voltage.
  2. I(6 Ω) = VR = 9.0 V6 Ω = 1.5 AOne branch: its own voltage and resistance.
  3. I(18 Ω) = 9.0 V18 Ω = 0.5 AThe bigger resistance takes the smaller current.
  4. Total I = 1.5 A + 0.5 A = 2.0 ABranch currents add up to the main wire current.
  5. 1R(total) = 16 + 118 = 318 + 118 = 418Use a common denominator.
  6. R(total) = 184 = 4.5 ΩFlip the fraction to get R.
  7. Check: 9.0 V4.5 Ω = 2.0 A, and 4.5 Ω is less than 6 ΩThe total matches the branch currents.

Answer: 1.5 A and 0.5 A; 2.0 A in total; R(total) = 4.5 Ω

Which thing? Branch currents use one branch's values. The 2.0 A and 4.5 Ω belong to the whole circuit.

Worksheet D · about 20 min · 11 questions

Current and voltage at different points

NameDate

Learning intention

To understand where current is the same, where it splits, and how voltage is shared, in different parts of a circuit.

Success criteria

  • I can predict what an ammeter reads at any point in a series or parallel circuit.
  • I can explain why the current going into a part equals the current coming out.
  • I can find the voltage across one part, or across a group of parts.
The rules

Current

  • An ammeter goes in series. It reads the current at that point.
  • Series: the same current at every point.
  • Parallel: the current splits at a junction and joins up again.
  • The current into a part equals the current out of it. Charge is not used up.

Voltage

  • A voltmeter goes in parallel, across a part.
  • Series: the voltages across the parts add up to the supply.
  • Parallel: each branch has the full supply voltage.

Worked example: Readings around a parallel circuit

An ammeter at P reads 0.6 A. An ammeter at Q reads 0.4 A. What would an ammeter read at W, at X and at Y?

  1. W: 0.4 AW is after lamp 1 in the same branch as Q. The current into a lamp equals the current out.
  2. X: 0.6 A − 0.4 A = 0.2 AP is in the main wire. The 0.6 A splits at the junction into the two branches.
  3. Y: 0.4 A + 0.2 A = 0.6 AThe branch currents join up again in the main wire.

Answer: W 0.4 A, X 0.2 A, Y 0.6 A

Which thing? P and Y are the whole circuit. Q and W are lamp 1's branch. X is lamp 2's branch.

Adding a resistor

NameDate

Learning intention

To understand why adding a resistor in series lowers the current, but adding one in parallel raises the supply current.

Success criteria

  • I can predict what happens to an ammeter reading when a resistor is added in series or in parallel.
  • I can calculate the current before and after a resistor is added.
  • I can explain changes in bulb brightness using resistance and current.
The rules

Adding in series

  • R(total) goes up.
  • The current goes down everywhere in the loop.
  • Bulbs in the loop get dimmer.

Adding in parallel

  • R(total) goes down, because there is another path.
  • The supply current goes up.
  • Each branch still gets the full voltage, so the other bulbs stay the same brightness.

Worked example: Adding a resistor in series

A 12 Ω bulb is joined to a 6.0 V battery. Then a 12 Ω resistor is added in series. Find the current before and after. What happens to the bulb?

  1. Before: I = VR = 6.0 V12 Ω = 0.5 AWhole circuit: only the bulb.
  2. After: R(total) = 12 Ω + 12 Ω = 24 ΩIn series, resistances add.
  3. After: I = 6.0 V24 Ω = 0.25 ASame supply voltage, more resistance.
  4. The current halves, so the bulb is dimmer.Less current through the bulb means less energy each second.

Answer: 0.5 A before, 0.25 A after. The bulb gets dimmer.

Which thing? Both currents use whole-circuit values. Adding the resistor changed the whole circuit, so R(total) changed too.

Mixed circuits

NameDate

Learning intention

To understand how to break a circuit into a series part and a parallel part, and match V, I and R in each.

Success criteria

  • I can replace a parallel pair with one resistance.
  • I can find the total current and share the supply voltage between the series part and the parallel pair.
  • I can find the current in each branch of the pair.
The rules

Plan for a mixed circuit

  • Find the resistance of the parallel pair.
  • Add the series resistor to get R(total).
  • Find the total current: I = V(supply)R(total).
  • Find the voltage across the series resistor, then what is left for the pair.
  • Find each branch current with the pair's voltage.

Worked example: A resistor in series with a parallel pair

R₁ = 3 Ω is in series with a parallel pair of 4 Ω and 12 Ω. The battery is 12 V. Find the total resistance, the total current and the current in each branch of the pair.

  1. Pair: 1R = 14 + 112 = 312 + 112 = 412, so R(pair) = 124 = 3 ΩReplace the pair with one resistance.
  2. R(total) = 3 Ω + 3 Ω = 6 ΩR₁ and the pair are in series.
  3. I = 12 V6 Ω = 2 AWhole circuit values.
  4. V(R₁) = 2 A × 3 Ω = 6 VAll 2 A flows through R₁.
  5. V(pair) = 12 V − 6 V = 6 VThe voltages add up to the supply.
  6. I(4 Ω) = 6 V4 Ω = 1.5 A and I(12 Ω) = 6 V12 Ω = 0.5 AUse the pair's 6 V, not the 12 V supply.
  7. Check: 1.5 A + 0.5 A = 2 AThe branch currents add up to the total.

Answer: 6 Ω; 2 A; 1.5 A in the 4 Ω and 0.5 A in the 12 Ω

Which thing? Total current: whole circuit. V(R₁): R₁ only. Branch currents: each branch, with the pair's 6 V.

Key words

current (I)
How much charge flows past a point each second. Measured in amps (A).
voltage (V)
How much energy each coulomb of charge gains or loses. Measured in volts (V).
resistance (R)
How much a part opposes the flow of charge. Measured in ohms (Ω).
series
Parts joined one after another in a single loop.
parallel
Parts joined in separate branches, side by side.
branch
One path in a parallel circuit.
junction
A point where the current splits or joins up.
main wire
The wire that carries the total current to and from the battery.
ammeter
Measures current. It goes in series, as part of the loop.
voltmeter
Measures voltage. It goes in parallel, across a part.
total resistance
The resistance of the whole circuit, R(total).

Watch out

  • Not quite: A bulb uses up current.

    Better: Current is the same into and out of a part. The bulb transfers energy, not charge.

  • Not quite: Use the supply voltage with any resistor.

    Better: Use the supply voltage only with the total resistance. For one part, use that part's own voltage.

  • Not quite: Adding a resistor always lowers the current.

    Better: In series, yes. In parallel, the supply current goes up, because there is another path.

  • Not quite: In parallel, the branches share the voltage.

    Better: Each branch gets the full supply voltage. The branches share the current.

  • Not quite: 1R(total) = 0.25, so R(total) = 0.25 Ω.

    Better: That is 1 over R. Flip it: R(total) = 10.25 = 4 Ω.
