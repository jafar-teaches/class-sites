Van de Graaff generator

Lesson 1: Static electricity and fieldsThu 29 OctP2 (9:51 am) · Room D19

Equipment

Ask before the demo

The volunteer's hair stands on end. Why do the strands push apart?

Answer: Every strand gets the same type of charge, so the strands repel.

Questions to ask

Why does the volunteer stand on an insulating stool?
So charge cannot flow away to the ground. It builds up on the volunteer.
Why do the pie tins fly off one at a time, starting at the top?
The top tin picks up charge from the dome and is repelled. Once it leaves, the next tin is on top, so it charges up and flies off.
Why does the fluorescent tube glow even though it is not plugged in?
The strong electric field near the dome makes the gas inside the tube glow.
A neutral object gains 5 electrons. What is its charge?
Negative. It has 5 more electrons than protons.

How to run it

  1. Trial it the day before, because belts perish and humidity weakens the effect. Clear phones, laptops and the casting device away from the dome.
  2. Ask the class about pacemakers and heart conditions. Wipe the dome and belt with the dry cloth.
  3. Volunteer (dry, fine hair works best) stands on the insulating stool and puts a hand on the dome BEFORE you switch on.
  4. Switch on. The hair stands up. Pause for the prediction vote: why do the strands push apart?
  5. Switch off. With the volunteer's hand still on the dome, touch the earthed wand to the dome (never to the volunteer). Then help them down.
  6. Stack the pie tins on the dome and switch on. They fly off one at a time.
  7. Hold the unplugged fluorescent tube near the dome. It glows.
  8. Bring the earthed wand towards the dome. A spark jumps to the wand. Switch off and discharge the dome.

The science

Friction between the belt and the rollers moves electrons, and the belt carries charge up to the dome, where it builds up on the outside. The volunteer touches the dome while insulated from the ground, so they share that charge. Every hair gets the same sign, so the strands repel each other and the head. Each pie tin picks up the dome's charge and is repelled, the top one first. The very strong electric field near the dome makes the gas in the tube glow. When enough charge builds up, the air between dome and wand breaks down and charge rushes across as a spark (a small lightning bolt). Whether your dome ends up positive or negative depends on the roller and belt materials. The explanation is the same either way.

Safety

  • No one with a pacemaker or heart condition takes part or stands close. Ask the class first.
  • The volunteer touches the dome before switch-on and keeps touching until you have switched off and discharged the dome with the wand.
  • Sparks go only to the discharge wand, never to people.
  • Keep electronics, laptops and the casting device away from the dome.
  • Hold the fluorescent tube by the glass at the far end, not the metal cap. If it breaks, clear the area (it contains mercury).
  • Balloons: check for latex allergies.

If it doesn't work

Humid days kill the effect. Wipe the dome and belt dry, run the machine for a minute, or warm the belt area with a hair dryer. Pie tins and the spark are more reliable than hair. If the machine fails completely, run the balloons (Explore) as the demo and cast the PhET simulation.

Bright bulb, dim bulb

Lesson 3: Resistance and Ohm's lawFri 6 NovP5 (1:55 pm) · Room D19

Equipment

Ask before the demo

A 10 Ω resistor is added to the loop with the bulb. What will happen?

Answer: The bulb gets dimmer, because less current flows.

Questions to ask

Did the current fall only after the resistor, or everywhere?
Everywhere in the loop. A single loop has the same current all the way round.
The voltage stays the same and the resistance gets bigger. What happens to the current?
It gets smaller (I = VR).
What happens to the current through a fixed resistor if you double the voltage?
It doubles.
Name two things that make a wire's resistance bigger.
Any two: longer, thinner, poorer conductor, hotter.

How to run it

  1. Build one loop: cells, switch, ammeter, bulb. Close the switch. The bulb glows brightly. Read the ammeter aloud (about 0.3 A).
  2. Open the switch. Students vote on the predict question.
  3. Add the 10 Ω resistor into the loop. Close the switch. The bulb is clearly dimmer and the ammeter reading drops (roughly 0.2 A).
  4. Optional: add a second 10 Ω resistor. The bulb dims again and the current drops further (roughly 0.15–0.17 A).
  5. Optional: move the ammeter to the other side of the resistor. The reading is the same, because the current is smaller everywhere in the loop.
  6. Remove the resistors and use 2 cells instead of 4. The bulb is also dimmer. Lowering the voltage and adding resistance both reduce the current.

The science

The bulb and resistor are in one loop with a fixed voltage. Adding resistance raises the total resistance, so by I = VR the current falls everywhere in the loop, and the bulb gets less energy each second. If the hot bulb were a fixed 20 Ω, the currents would be 620 = 0.30 A, 630 = 0.20 A and 640 = 0.15 A. The real bulb's resistance falls as it cools, so the dimmer readings come out a little higher. Don't promise exact numbers.

Safety

  • 6 V DC or less.
  • Use the 5 W resistor. A 0.5 W resistor is not safe here.
  • The bulb and resistor get hot. Switch off between steps and don't touch them while current flows.

If it doesn't work

If the dimming is hard to see, darken the room or use 2 cells (3 V) so the bulb starts less bright, and rely on the ammeter reading on the visualiser.

Ohm's law bench

Lesson 4: Practical 1: Ohm's lawMon 9 NovP3 (11:21 am) · Room D22

Equipment

Ask before the demo

A 10 Ω resistor has 3 V across it and a current of 0.30 A. The voltage is doubled to 6 V. What will the current be?

Answer: About 0.60 A

Questions to ask

Which is the independent variable today?
The voltage across the resistor.
Why do we switch off between readings?
So the resistor does not heat up. A hotter resistor has more resistance, which would change a controlled variable.
Why take three readings at each voltage?
To spot mistakes and calculate a mean, which is more reliable.
Your cells read less than 1.5 V each. Does that spoil the test?
No. We measure the actual voltage with the voltmeter and use that value.

How to run it

  1. Build the circuit in front of the class with one cell. Close the switch and read both meters aloud.
  2. Open the switch. Add a second cell. Ask the class to predict the new current, then close the switch.
  3. Read both meters again. Don't name the rule yet. The practical will test it.

The science

For an ohmic resistor at constant temperature, current is proportional to voltage (I = VR). One cell gives about 1.44 V and 0.14 A; two cells about 2.80 V and 0.28 A. The cells' own internal resistance pulls the voltage a little under 1.5 V per cell, which is why we always use the voltmeter reading.

Safety

  • 6 V DC or less.
  • Use a resistor rated at least 5 W. Switch off between readings.
  • Resistors and bulbs get hot. Don't touch them while current flows.
  • Report damaged leads or a hot smell.

If it doesn't work

If the ammeter reads 0, check the switch and that the ammeter is in the loop (not across the resistor). If the voltmeter reads 0, check it is across the resistor, not across a lead.

Two resistors, one battery

Lesson 5: Ohm's law problems: worked examples and practiceTue 10 NovP2 (9:51 am) · Room E08

Equipment

Ask before the demo

A 3 V battery is joined to a 100 Ω resistor, then to a 1 kΩ (1,000 Ω) resistor. How do the currents compare?

Answer: The 100 Ω resistor has 10 times the current

Questions to ask

What is 30 mA in amps?
0.030 A (divide by 1,000).
Why does the 1 kΩ resistor give a tenth of the current?
It has ten times the resistance, and I = VR with the same V.
Which arrangement do you use to find R?
R = VI.
On the graph, how can you tell which line is the bigger resistance?
With current on the y-axis, the less steep line (less current per volt) is the bigger resistance.

Or run it as a simulation

No resistors or meter? Run the same demo on screen with the PhET Ohm's Law simulation, embedded at the top of the lesson's Explore section. Set 3.0 V and 100 Ω (30 mA), then 1,000 Ω (3 mA).

Open it in Lesson 5 → · Open the simulation on its own ↗

How to run it

  1. Show the two resistors and read the labels (or colour bands) aloud: 100 Ω and 1 kΩ.
  2. Ask the prediction question and take a vote.
  3. Connect the 100 Ω resistor in series with the meter. Read about 30 mA.
  4. Swap in the 1 kΩ resistor. Read about 3 mA.
  5. Write 30 mA = 0.030 A and 3 mA = 0.003 A on the board to practise today's unit conversions.

The science

I = VR: 3100 = 0.03 A and 31,000 = 0.003 A. Ten times the resistance gives one tenth of the current. Readings may be a touch lower if the cells are tired. Power is tiny (0.09 W in the 100 Ω), so small resistors are safe.

Safety

  • 3 V DC only.
  • Never put the meter on the 10 A or mA range directly across the cells.

If it doesn't work

If the meter reads 0, check it is on the mA range with the lead in the mA socket and that it is in series. If the mA fuse inside the meter has blown, use the 10 A range for the 100 Ω reading only (it may read 0.03) and explain the conversion anyway.

Unscrew one bulb: series and parallel

Lesson 6: Practical 2: Series and parallelThu 12 NovP2 (9:51 am) · Room D19

Equipment

Ask before the demo

You join three identical bulbs to the power pack, first in series, then in parallel. Which way takes more current from the supply?

Answer: Three bulbs in parallel

Questions to ask

How many paths does a series circuit have?
One.
Where must the ammeter go to measure the total current?
In the main wire, between the battery and the first junction, where all the current passes.
Why build a one-bulb reference circuit first?
So there is something to compare brightness and current with.
What happens to the total current as you add bulbs in parallel?
It goes up by about one bulb's current each time.

How to run it

  1. Switch on both circuits. Ask: which bulbs look brighter? (Parallel.)
  2. Unscrew one bulb in the series circuit. All the bulbs go out and the ammeter drops to 0.
  3. Unscrew one bulb in the parallel circuit. The others stay on and the ammeter drops by about one bulb's current.
  4. Point at the two ammeters: 'Which battery is working harder? Write your prediction.'

The science

Series has one path, so a break stops current everywhere. Parallel branches each make their own loop through the battery. The ammeters already hint at today's answer (about 0.15 A vs 0.83 A), so keep the focus on brightness and broken bulbs until after the vote.

Safety

  • 3 V DC only.
  • Switch off before unscrewing. Bulbs get hot.
  • Never put a wire straight across the cells. This short circuit makes the cells and wires hot.

If it doesn't work

If the three series bulbs don't glow on 3 V (1.0 V each), say so and point out that the ammeter still shows current. Use 2 bulbs in series instead, or a 4.5 V supply for the series demo only. Never use 4.5 V for the parallel circuit, because it overruns the 3.5 V bulbs.

One bulb, then two bulbs in parallel

Lesson 7: Calculating in series and parallel circuitsMon 16 NovP4 (12:21 pm) · Room D22

Equipment

Ask before the demo

Two identical bulbs are joined in parallel. Is their total resistance more or less than one bulb on its own?

Answer: Less, because there are two paths for the current

Questions to ask

In the demo, why did the current go up when we added a bulb?
The new bulb is on its own branch with the full 3 V, so it takes its own current. The battery supplies both.
In series, which resistor gets more voltage?
The bigger resistance, because the same current flows through both and V = I × R.
What is the total resistance of 6 Ω and 3 Ω in parallel, and how can you check it?
2 Ω. Check: 122 = 6 A, the same as 2 A + 4 A.
A student gets 1R = 0.5 and writes R = 0.5 Ω. What went wrong?
They forgot to flip: R = 10.5 = 2 Ω.

How to run it

  1. Build one bulb on 3 V with the ammeter in the main wire. The ammeter reads about 0.28 A and the voltmeter about 3.0 V.
  2. Ask the prediction question and take a vote.
  3. Add a second bulb on its own branch. Read about 0.55 A.
  4. Students calculate R = VI both times: about 10.7 Ω, then about 5.5 Ω.

The science

Each branch gets the full voltage and takes its own current, so the battery's current roughly doubles. With the same voltage and double the current, the total resistance has halved. The new path does not 'add' resistance. It adds another way through, and it doesn't change the first branch.

Safety

  • 3 V DC only.
  • Bulbs get hot. Switch off before changing the circuit.

If it doesn't work

If the current doesn't roughly double, the cells may be tired (their voltage sags at bigger currents). Read the voltmeter too and use the measured voltage in R = VI. Or use a 3 V power pack.

Homopolar motor, then a magnet falling through a copper pipe

Lesson 8: Electromagnetism: the homopolar motor and the copper pipeTue 17 NovP2 (9:51 am) · Room D22

Equipment

Ask before the demo

A strong magnet is dropped down a copper pipe. Copper does not stick to magnets. What will the magnet do?

Answer: Fall through much more slowly than the slug.

Questions to ask

Why did the homopolar wire only spin while it touched the magnet?
It needs a closed circuit. The current flows down the wire and back through the magnet. Without current there is no force.
Why did flipping the magnet reverse the spin?
The magnet's field now points the other way, so the force on the current reverses.
Is copper magnetic? How do you know?
No. A magnet does not stick to it, and the plain slug test shows the pipe itself does nothing special to non-magnets.
Why would a pipe with a slit let the magnet fall faster?
The slit breaks the loops of current round the pipe, so the currents and their opposing field are much weaker.

How to run it

  1. Homopolar: stand the cell on the disc magnet, negative end down.
  2. Shape the wire so it balances on the top (+) terminal and lightly brushes the side of the magnet. Let go and it spins.
  3. Lift it off after a few seconds (it short-circuits the cell). Flip the magnet over and repeat. It spins the other way.
  4. Pipe: hold it upright over the cushion. Drop the plain slug; students time it.
  5. Take the class vote on the prediction. Drop the neodymium magnet; students time it (several seconds).
  6. Optional: drop the magnet down the slit pipe. It falls faster, because the currents can't flow all the way round.
  7. Leave the pipe as a mystery until board step 7.

The science

Homopolar: current flows from the + terminal down the wire, through the magnet's conductive nickel coating, and back into the − terminal. Near the magnet the current runs sideways (outwards from the centre) while the magnet's field runs up and down, so the field pushes sideways on the current and the wire spins (the motor effect). Flipping the magnet reverses the field, so the force and the spin reverse. Copper pipe: as the magnet falls, the magnetic field through each ring of copper changes, which drives loops of current (eddy currents) in the copper. By Lenz's law their field opposes the change. The ring above pulls the magnet back and the ring below repels it. The magnet quickly reaches a low steady speed. Its lost gravitational energy becomes heat in the copper (too little to feel). The slug falls freely, taking about 0.45 s for 1 m, 0.55 s for 1.5 m, 0.64 s for 2 m.

Safety

  • The homopolar motor short-circuits the cell, so the wire and cell get hot. Run it in short bursts only.
  • Neodymium magnets pinch hard and snap together. Keep them apart and away from phones, cards and laptops.
  • Catch the falling magnet on a cushion. A chipped neodymium magnet has sharp edges.
  • Strong neodymium magnets: no one with a pacemaker or other implant handles them or stands close. Ask the class first.
  • Homopolar motor: alkaline AA cells only, never lithium or rechargeable cells (much higher short-circuit current, can overheat or vent).

If it doesn't work

Homopolar: use a fresh cell, clean the magnet's surface, and reshape the wire so it touches the magnet lightly (too much friction stops it). Pipe: the magnet must be strong (neodymium) and the pipe copper or aluminium, not steel or plastic. A thicker-walled pipe gives a slower fall.
